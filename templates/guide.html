The two modes

Pick one at the start of every session. They are judged differently.

Impromptu

Think on your feet

The topic appears and the clock starts. Rambling is normal here, so loose structure and thin development are weighted down. What counts is staying on topic, not going blank, and not repeating yourself.

Prepared

Plan, then deliver

Two, five or ten minutes with a five-box scaffold — opening line, three points, closing line. The notes vanish the moment the speaking timer starts. Here, weak structure and a missing ending are preparation failures worth naming.

One speech, step by step

About two minutes, start to finish.

01Choose impromptu or prepared, and how long to speak — 60, 90 or 120 seconds
02A topic appears for your child's age band. Ask for a different one as often as you like; none repeats until the pool runs out
03In prepared mode, fill the five note boxes. They disappear when you start speaking
04Press start, wait for the three-two-one, then speak until the ring runs out or you press "I'm done"
05Read the feedback: one strength, one thing to try. The full report sits on the second tab

Half one

What they said

Seven dimensions, scored one to five from the transcript. Weighted differently for prepared and impromptu speeches.

  • Specificity — concrete details and named examples, versus abstractions
  • Personal stake — an experience or opinion they actually own
  • Reasoning — claims backed with a because, versus asserted and dropped
  • Angle — their own way in, versus the obvious take
  • Development — one idea explored, versus five touched and abandoned
  • On topic and structure — did they stay with it, open and close

Idea dimensions, out of 5

Half two

How they said it

Computed from the waveform and the word timings. No language model is asked how someone sounded, so the same recording always gives the same numbers.

  • Pace — words a minute. Under 90 is hesitant, over 170 is rushing
  • Pauses — every gap over 0.7 seconds, and crucially where they cluster
  • Filler words — um, uh, like, so, basically, matlab, yaani
  • Repetition — repeated two and three word phrases
  • Vocabulary — range, and words outside the thousand most common
  • Pitch and volume — against your child's own baseline, never a fixed chart

Questions

Why does it say "calibrating" for pitch and volume?

A seven year old's voice is naturally much higher and quieter than a thirteen year old's, so judging either against a fixed threshold would tell every young child they are shrieking. Instead the first three speeches become that child's own baseline. Until those exist, both metrics are marked calibrating and generate no feedback at all.

Why only one thing to work on?

A child given five corrections fixes none of them. The priority order means the biggest problem is always addressed first, and once it improves the next one surfaces on its own.

What happens to the recording?

It is transcribed and analysed, then deleted from the server immediately. A playback copy stays in this browser on this device. The transcript and the measurements are kept so the dashboard can show progress.

Can I add more than one child?

Yes, up to eight. Each profile keeps its own history, streak, topic rotation and voice baseline, so siblings sharing one computer never affect each other's feedback.

Does filler word counting always work?

Speech recognition sometimes drops disfluencies during decoding. When no fillers are detected at all, the report says so rather than reporting a confident zero, because the absence is weak evidence rather than proof.

That is the whole thing.

The rest is practice. Most children never speak uninterrupted for a full minute.
